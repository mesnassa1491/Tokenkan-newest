1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1.1 Overview of the Issuer

Sajian Warisan Bhd operates and franchises a chain of halal casual dining restaurants in Malaysia. It operates 34 outlets directly and
franchises a further 22 to sub-franchisees, and supplies both networks with sauces, marinades and prepared components from a central
kitchen it owns at Selayang, Selangor.

The Issuer has been listed on the ACE Market of Bursa Malaysia since November 2021 and had a market capitalisation of RM68.4 million
as at 30 June 2026. It employed 486 persons as at 31 December 2025.

For the financial year ended 31 December 2025 the Issuer recorded revenue of RM51.8 million, EBITDA of RM12.66 million and profit for
the financial year of RM3.70 million. Total assets stood at RM78.7 million and shareholders' funds at RM23.0 million.

1.2 Purpose of the offering

The Issuer is offering RM4,000,000 in nominal value of Sukuk Murabahah, the inaugural issue under its RM20.0 million Sukuk Programme.
Of that, RM2,600,000 is allocated to the Anchor Investor and RM1,400,000 to retail investors through the Tokenkan platform in fractional
denominations from RM100, subject to a maximum holding of RM10,000 per investor.

Proceeds will fund the fit-out of six new outlets, central kitchen equipment, the refinancing of existing equipment financing and working
capital. Section 5 has the detail.

1.3 Why this issuance exists at all

The economic case for this offering is worth stating plainly, because it explains both the opportunity and the risk.

A conventional RM4.0 million bond issuance is impossible in Malaysia. Rating, trustee, legal, Shariah advisory, registry, settlement and
underwriting fees total RM1.4 million to RM1.9 million largely irrespective of deal size, which on RM4.0 million would consume 35 to 48
per cent. of the proceeds. Companies of this size therefore have two options: dilute their equity, or borrow from a bank against security on
terms the bank sets.

The total cost of this issuance is RM180,000, being 4.5 per cent. of nominal value. That reduction is achieved by removing the underwriter,
whose distribution function the retail base performs in aggregate, by declining to obtain a rating, and by standardising the documentation
and the registry and paying agency functions.

Investors should understand what they are accepting in exchange. There is no rating. There is no underwriter who has put capital at risk
behind the credit. The Issuer is a small company in a sector with high failure rates. The compensation is a profit rate of 7.40 per cent., the
highest on the Platform, and security over the central kitchen premises.

1.4 What protects investors in the absence of a rating

Three things, none of which is equivalent to a rating:

    Security. A first-ranking charge over the central kitchen premises valued at RM11.4 million, a debenture over the assets of the Issuer,
    an assignment of RM8.2 million of annual franchise royalties, and a corporate guarantee from the controlling shareholder. Section 4.6
    sets out the security and assesses candidly what it is likely to be worth.
    Covenants. Gearing, leverage, coverage, shareholders' funds and security cover tests, in Section 4.8, monitored semi-annually by an
    independent trustee.
    Anchor Investor participation. A professional credit fund conducted its own credit assessment and took 65 per cent. of the issue at
    the same price and on the same terms as retail investors, ranking equally with them.

2. ISSUER BACKGROUND

2.1 History and development

The Issuer was incorporated in June 2015 by Rosli bin Hamid, who had spent the preceding fourteen years in multi-outlet restaurant
operations, to acquire the Malaysian master franchise for the casual dining brand it now operates. The first outlet opened at Kepong in
2016.

The Issuer opened outlets steadily through 2017 to 2020, acquired the Selayang central kitchen premises in 2019, and began sub-
franchising in 2019 as a means of extending the network without funding fit-out from its own balance sheet. It listed on the ACE Market in
November 2021, raising RM14.2 million, of which RM9.8 million funded outlet expansion.

The East Malaysia operations were established in 2023 through a seventy per cent. owned subsidiary with a local partner holding the
balance.

This is the inaugural debt issuance of the Issuer. It has not previously accessed the debt capital markets, and the reason is set out in
Section 1.3.

2.2 Business model

The Issuer earns revenue in three ways. It operates 34 restaurants directly, taking the full margin and bearing the full cost. It franchises 22
territories to sub-franchisees, from whom it receives an initial franchise fee and a continuing royalty calculated on their sales. And it sells
prepared components from its central kitchen to both networks at a margin.

The distinction matters to a creditor. Own-outlet revenue of RM38.6 million in FY2025 is larger but capital-intensive and operationally
exposed: the Issuer funds the fit-out, signs the lease and employs the staff. Franchise and central kitchen income of RM12.4 million is
smaller but requires little capital, carries no lease obligation, and survives the closure of an individual sub-franchised outlet because the
sub-franchisee bears that loss. It is also the income stream assigned to the Trustee as security under Section 4.6.

The Issuer operates the brand under a master franchise agreement with six years remaining. That agreement, and not any physical asset,
is the foundation of the business, and its loss is the risk investors should weigh most heavily.

4.6 Security and collateral

Unusually for a small issue, and deliberately, these Sukuk are secured. The Platform required security as a condition of originating an

The security comprises:

    a first-ranking charge over the central kitchen premises at Kawasan Perindustrian Selayang, a freehold industrial property valued at

4.7 Ranking of investors

The Sukuk constitute direct, unconditional and unsubordinated obligations of the Issuer, secured as described in Section 4.6 and ranking
pari passu among themselves.

To the extent of the security, the Sukuk rank ahead of the unsecured creditors of the Issuer. On the central kitchen premises specifically,
the Sukuk rank behind existing Islamic term financing of RM7.2 million charged on the same property.

In respect of any shortfall not recovered from the security, Sukukholders would rank as unsecured creditors, behind the costs of a winding-

Landlords hold statutory and contractual rights in respect of leased outlet premises which rank ahead of the floating charge in respect of
assets at those premises.

Fractional interests held through the Platform rank equally with whole units, and equally with the allocation of the Anchor Investor.

7.1 Risks relating to the Issuer and the Sukuk

Credit risk

The Sukuk represent unsecured or, where applicable, secured obligations of the Issuer alone. Payment of profit and the repayment of the
nominal value depend entirely upon the ability of the Issuer to generate sufficient cash flow from its operations, or to obtain sufficient
funds from other sources, at the relevant times. Neither the Trustee, the Platform through which this offering was originated and
distributed, nor any other party has guaranteed or assumed any obligation in respect of the payment obligations of the Issuer. In
particular, investors should note that this offering was not underwritten and that no arranger stands behind it.

A deterioration in the operating performance of the Issuer, an adverse change in its capital structure, the loss of one or more significant
customers or contracts, or an unexpected increase in its cost base could impair its ability to service the Sukuk. As at 31 December 2025,
the Group had total borrowings of RM32.0 million against total equity of RM23.0 million, representing a gross gearing ratio of 1.39 times
and total borrowings to EBITDA of 2.5 times.

Refinancing risk

The Sukuk are repayable in a single bullet payment at maturity, and the Issuer does not expect to accumulate a sinking fund sufficient to
retire the full nominal amount from operating cash flow alone. The Issuer therefore expects to refinance all or a substantial part of the
Sukuk at or before maturity. The ability of the Issuer to refinance will depend on conditions in the Malaysian debt capital and bank loan
markets at the relevant time, on its credit standing and rating at that time, and on investor appetite for the sector in which it operates.
There is no assurance that refinancing will be available on acceptable terms, or at all.

Profit rate risk

The Sukuk carry a fixed profit rate. The market value of fixed-rate debt securities generally moves inversely with prevailing market yields:
if benchmark yields, including the yields on Malaysian Government Securities and Government Investment Issues, rise after the issue date,
the market value of the Sukuk is likely to fall. Investors who sell the Sukuk before maturity may therefore realise less than the nominal
value, and in adverse conditions materially less. Investors who hold the Sukuk to maturity are not exposed to this price risk, but bear the
opportunity cost of being locked into a fixed profit rate for the tenure of the instrument.

The Issuer is separately exposed to profit rate risk on its floating-rate bank borrowings, which are typically priced against the relevant cost
of funds of the lending institutions. An increase in the Overnight Policy Rate set by the central bank would increase the finance costs of the
Group and reduce the headroom available under its profit cover covenant.

Liquidity risk of the Sukuk

The Sukuk will not be listed or quoted on any stock exchange, and the Platform does not operate an order book. Operating a venue that
matches buyers and sellers of capital market products would require registration as a Recognized Market Operator, which the Platform has
not yet obtained. Exit before maturity is therefore available only through the standing bid maintained by the Anchor Investor at the
published formula price described in Section 9.5. That bid is a contractual undertaking of a single counterparty, not a market. It is capped
at RM70,000 of nominal value in aggregate in any calendar month, may be suspended in the circumstances set out in Section 9.5, and
terminates if the Anchor Investor ceases to hold at least half of its original allocation. Peer-to-peer transfer between investors is intended
to follow in phase two once Recognized Market Operator registration is obtained, but no date is committed and investors should not
subscribe in reliance on it. An investor who may need the money before 15 September 2029 should treat this offering as unsuitable.

Liquidity and working capital risk of the Issuer

The Issuer requires liquidity to fund its operations, service its debt and meet its capital commitments. As at 31 December 2025, the Group
held cash and bank balances of RM9.1 million and recorded a current ratio of 1.01 times. An unexpected lengthening of the collection
cycle, the withdrawal of uncommitted banking lines, or a requirement to post additional performance security could strain the liquidity
position of the Group.

Rating risk

No rating agency has been appointed and these Sukuk are unrated. Investors therefore have no independent professional opinion on the
creditworthiness of the Issuer, no continuous surveillance of that opinion, and no downgrade signal to warn them of deterioration. On a
rated issue, a rating agency reviews the credit at least annually and will place it on watch or downgrade it if conditions worsen, and that
signal is public. Here there is no such mechanism. Investors depend instead on the semi-annual covenant compliance certificates delivered
to the Trustee under Section 4.8, on the quarterly reporting the Issuer has undertaken to publish through the Platform, and on the
continuing disclosure obligations to which the Issuer is subject as an ACE Market listed company. Those are materially less than a rating
provides.

7.2 Risks relating to the industry and operations of the Issuer

Dependence on a single master franchise agreement
The Issuer operates its principal brand under a master franchise agreement for Malaysia granted by an overseas franchisor, with a
remaining term of six years and one renewal option exercisable at the discretion of the franchisor. That agreement is the foundation of the
business: it confers the brand, the recipes, the operating system and the right to sub-franchise.

Termination would end the greater part of the business. The agreement is terminable by the franchisor for breach of operating standards,
failure to meet minimum outlet targets, or insolvency of the Issuer, and the Issuer has received one formal notice of non-compliance during
the review period, relating to store standards at two outlets, which was remedied within the cure period.

The Sukuk carry a mandatory redemption on loss of the master franchise, described in Section 4.5, but investors should understand that a
mandatory redemption obligation is only as good as the ability of the Issuer to pay it, which in that scenario would be doubtful.

Fixed occupancy and staff cost against variable sales

Restaurant economics are unforgiving in a downturn. Rent under outlet leases is fixed for the lease term, staff must be rostered to cover
opening hours regardless of footfall, and depreciation on fit-out continues whether an outlet is busy or empty. Sales, by contrast, move with
consumer spending, weather, competing openings and the fortunes of the mall or street the outlet sits in.

The Issuer operates 34 own outlets under leases with a weighted average remaining term of 3.4 years. Occupancy and staff costs together
represented 61.8 per cent. of own-outlet revenue in FY2025. A decline of fifteen per cent. in same-outlet sales, which is well within the
range observed in Malaysian food service during periods of weak consumer sentiment, would eliminate the greater part of own-outlet
contribution.

Input cost inflation

Food input costs are volatile and largely outside the control of the Issuer. Chicken, cooking oil, flour, dairy and packaging have all seen
double-digit swings in Malaysia over the past five years, some of them driven by import dependence and exchange rates rather than
domestic conditions.

Menu prices cannot be raised freely in response. Price increases in the mid-market segment in which the Issuer operates provoke
immediate volume loss, and franchise agreements constrain pricing latitude. The gross margin of the Issuer improved from 58.6 per cent.
to 59.7 per cent. over the review period, but that improvement reflects scale in central kitchen purchasing rather than pricing power and
would reverse under input cost pressure.

Delivery platform dependence and commission

Delivery channels contributed 28.4 per cent. of own-outlet revenue in FY2025. Delivery aggregators charge commissions in the range of
twenty to thirty per cent. of order value, which is materially higher than the contribution margin on a dine-in order, so delivery revenue is
considerably less profitable than its headline suggests.

The Issuer has no negotiating leverage over commission rates and no control over the algorithms determining its visibility on those
platforms. A commission increase, or a reduction in placement, would affect revenue directly. Concentration on a small number of
aggregators compounds this.

Sub-franchisee failure and royalty default
Franchise fees, royalties and central kitchen sales to 22 sub-franchisees contributed RM12.4 million in FY2025 and are the income stream
assigned to the Trustee as security. That income depends on sub-franchisees remaining solvent and trading.

Sub-franchisees are typically individuals or small partnerships with limited capital, and three ceased operating during the review period.
Recovery of amounts owed by a failed sub-franchisee is generally poor, and the Issuer bears the cost of either finding a replacement
operator or writing off the territory.
