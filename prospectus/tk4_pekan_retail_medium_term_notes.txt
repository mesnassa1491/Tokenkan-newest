1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1.1 Overview of the Issuer

Pekan Retail Assets Bhd owns and operates two neighbourhood shopping centres in Selangor: Pekan Bandar Baru Mall at Bandar Baru
Bangi and Pekan Semenyih Mall at Semenyih. Aggregate net lettable area is 386,000 square feet.

Both centres are anchored by supermarket operators and weighted toward groceries, food and beverage, pharmacy, clinics, tuition centres
and services rather than toward fashion or destination retail. Portfolio occupancy was 92.8 per cent. as at 31 December 2025.

The Issuer has been listed on the Main Market of Bursa Malaysia since August 2015 and had a market capitalisation of RM108.4 million as
at 30 June 2026. It employed 64 persons as at 31 December 2025; centre operations staff are engaged through service contractors.

For the financial year ended 31 December 2025 the Issuer recorded revenue of RM34.2 million, EBITDA of RM17.26 million and profit for
the financial year of RM4.48 million, excluding fair value movements. Total assets stood at RM249.8 million and shareholders' funds at
RM92.6 million.

1.2 Purpose of the offering

The Issuer is offering RM6,000,000 in nominal value of Senior Unsecured Medium Term Notes, the inaugural issue under its RM30.0
million programme. Of that, RM3,900,000 is allocated to the Anchor Investor and RM2,100,000 to retail investors through the Tokenkan
platform in fractional denominations from RM100, subject to a maximum holding of RM20,000 per investor.

Proceeds will fund asset enhancement works at both centres, solar photovoltaic installation and chiller replacement, refinancing and
working capital. Section 5 has the detail.

1.3 How a property investment company repays debt, and why it matters

This is the point investors most often misunderstand about property bonds, so it is stated plainly here.

The Issuer does not repay principal out of earnings. Rental income services interest and funds distributions; principal is refinanced at
maturity against the value of the properties. There is no scenario in which RM6.0 million of accumulated profit retires this issue in 2030,
and none is contemplated.

Repayment therefore depends on two things that cannot be assessed today: what the centres are worth in 2030, and whether credit is
available then. Both were favourable in 2015 when the Issuer listed and in 2021 when it last refinanced. Neither is guaranteed for 2030.

What investors have instead of amortisation is asset cover, tested by covenant. Investment properties are carried at RM229.8 million
against total borrowings of RM125.8 million, the unencumbered asset covenant requires 130 per cent. cover of unsecured debt and
currently stands at 162 per cent., and an independent valuation must be obtained at least every two years and reported to the Trustee.
Section 4.6 sets out how those work.

1.4 Why this issuance exists

A conventional RM6.0 million bond issuance is not economic. Fixed costs of RM1.4 million to RM1.9 million, largely irrespective of size,
would consume 23 to 32 per cent. of the proceeds. The total cost of this issuance is RM170,000, being 2.83 per cent. of nominal value.

2. ISSUER BACKGROUND

2.1 History and development

The Issuer was incorporated in August 2009 by Yap Beng Hoe, who had previously managed shopping centres for a regional operator, to
acquire an underperforming centre at Bandar Baru Bangi. That centre, refurbished and re-tenanted between 2010 and 2013, remains the
larger of the two assets.

Pekan Semenyih Mall was acquired in 2017 from a developer following completion. The Issuer has followed a consistent approach of
acquiring existing neighbourhood centres in established residential catchments at a discount to replacement cost and improving them,
rather than developing new centres, which management regards as carrying development risk disproportionate to the returns available in
Malaysian retail.

The Issuer listed on the Main Market of Bursa Malaysia in August 2015, raising RM32.6 million. This is its inaugural debt capital markets
issuance; it has funded itself to date through equity and bank term loans.

2.2 Business model

The Issuer earns rental income from tenants under leases of typically three years, supplemented by turnover rent linked to tenant sales,
car park income and advertising income. Property operating costs are substantially recovered from tenants through service charges.

The economics turn on occupancy and rent per square foot, both of which depend on footfall, which depends in turn on the tenant mix and
on the anchor tenants who draw visitors. This creates the dependency described in Section 7.2: anchors occupy large areas at below-
average rents, and their value lies in the specialty rents their footfall supports rather than in the rent they themselves pay.

A neighbourhood centre differs from a regional mall in a way that matters to a creditor. Its catchment is the surrounding residential
population rather than a wide travel-to-shop area, its tenant mix is weighted to necessity rather than discretionary spending, and its
performance is therefore less volatile but also less capable of growth. Both characteristics are visible in the figures: revenue growth of
around nine per cent. annually, and occupancy that has not fallen below 89 per cent.

2.5 Management and employees

The Issuer employed 64 persons directly as at 31 December 2025, comprising centre management, leasing, marketing, finance and
administration. Security, cleaning, landscaping and technical maintenance are provided by service contractors rather than by direct
employees, which is why the headcount is low relative to the size of the asset base.

2.6 Strategy

The strategy has three elements: to raise occupancy at Semenyih toward the level achieved at Bandar Baru through re-tenanting; to
complete the asset enhancement programme at both centres, which this issue part-funds; and to reduce borrowings to EBITDA below 6.5
times over the medium term. The Issuer does not intend to develop new centres or to acquire further assets until leverage has been
reduced.

4.4 Maturity structure

The Notes are structured as a single bullet maturity. The full nominal value of RM6,000,000 falls due on 5 November 2030 together with
the final coupon. Investors should understand plainly that a property investment company does not repay debt out of earnings. Rental
income services interest and funds distributions; principal is refinanced at maturity against the value of the properties. Repayment
therefore depends on property values and credit availability in 2030, neither of which can be assessed today. This is the central risk in
these Notes and is discussed in Section 7.2.

4.5 Early redemption

The Bonds incorporate the following early redemption provisions:

Optional early redemption (call)
The Issuer may, on any coupon payment date falling on or after the second anniversary of the issue date, redeem all but not part only of the
Notes on not less than thirty days' notice, at 100.75 per cent. of nominal value before the third anniversary and 100.35 per cent. thereafter,
together with accrued and unpaid coupon.

Mandatory redemption on disposal of a centre
If the Issuer disposes of either Pekan Bandar Baru Mall or Pekan Semenyih Mall, the net proceeds shall be applied within ninety days to
the redemption of Notes at nominal value together with accrued coupon, unless the Trustee is satisfied that the proceeds will be applied
within twelve months to the acquisition of a replacement investment property of comparable value. Given that the Issuer owns only two
material assets, investors should treat this as a substantive protection.

Redemption following a change of control
Upon a change of control, being an event whereby Pekan Holdings Sdn Bhd ceases to hold, directly or indirectly, more than thirty per cent.
of the voting shares of the Issuer, each Bondholder may require redemption at nominal value together with accrued coupon within sixty
days.

Redemption for taxation reasons
The Issuer may redeem all but not part only of the Notes at nominal value together with accrued coupon if a change in Malaysian tax law
obliges it to pay additional amounts.

No investor put
Save on a change of control, investors have no right to require redemption before maturity. Exit before maturity is available only through
the Anchor Investor standing bid described in Section 9.5.

The Notes constitute direct, unconditional, unsecured and unsubordinated obligations of the Issuer, ranking pari passu among themselves
and with its other unsecured and unsubordinated obligations, save for obligations mandatorily preferred by law.

Tenant security deposits held by the Issuer, amounting to RM11.8 million as at 31 December 2025, are repayable to tenants and are not
available to Bondholders.

Fractional interests rank equally with whole units and with the Anchor Investor allocation.

7.1 Risks relating to the Issuer and the Bonds

Credit risk
The Bonds represent unsecured or, where applicable, secured obligations of the Issuer alone. Payment of coupon and the repayment of the
nominal value depend entirely upon the ability of the Issuer to generate sufficient cash flow from its operations, or to obtain sufficient
funds from other sources, at the relevant times. Neither the Trustee, the Platform through which this offering was originated and
distributed, nor any other party has guaranteed or assumed any obligation in respect of the payment obligations of the Issuer. In
particular, investors should note that this offering was not underwritten and that no arranger stands behind it.

A deterioration in the operating performance of the Issuer, an adverse change in its capital structure, the loss of one or more significant
customers or contracts, or an unexpected increase in its cost base could impair its ability to service the Bonds. As at 31 December 2025,
the Group had total borrowings of RM125.8 million against total equity of RM92.6 million, representing a gross gearing ratio of 1.36 times
and total borrowings to EBITDA of 7.3 times.

Refinancing risk
The Bonds are repayable in a single bullet payment at maturity, and the Issuer does not expect to accumulate a sinking fund sufficient to
retire the full nominal amount from operating cash flow alone. The Issuer therefore expects to refinance all or a substantial part of the
Bonds at or before maturity. The ability of the Issuer to refinance will depend on conditions in the Malaysian debt capital and bank loan
markets at the relevant time, on its credit standing and rating at that time, and on investor appetite for the sector in which it operates.
There is no assurance that refinancing will be available on acceptable terms, or at all.

Interest rate risk
The Bonds carry a fixed coupon rate. The market value of fixed-rate debt securities generally moves inversely with prevailing market
yields: if benchmark yields, including the yields on Malaysian Government Securities, rise after the issue date, the market value of the
Bonds is likely to fall. Investors who sell the Bonds before maturity may therefore realise less than the nominal value, and in adverse
conditions materially less. Investors who hold the Bonds to maturity are not exposed to this price risk, but bear the opportunity cost of
being locked into a fixed coupon rate for the tenure of the instrument.

The Issuer is separately exposed to coupon rate risk on its floating-rate bank borrowings, which are typically priced against the relevant
cost of funds of the lending institutions. An increase in the Overnight Policy Rate set by the central bank would increase the finance costs
of the Group and reduce the headroom available under its coupon cover covenant.

Liquidity risk of the Bonds
The Bonds will not be listed or quoted on any stock exchange, and the Platform does not operate an order book. Operating a venue that
matches buyers and sellers of capital market products would require registration as a Recognized Market Operator, which the Platform has
not yet obtained. Exit before maturity is therefore available only through the standing bid maintained by the Anchor Investor at the
published formula price described in Section 9.5. That bid is a contractual undertaking of a single counterparty, not a market. It is capped
at RM105,000 of nominal value in aggregate in any calendar month, may be suspended in the circumstances set out in Section 9.5, and
terminates if the Anchor Investor ceases to hold at least half of its original allocation. Peer-to-peer transfer between investors is intended
to follow in phase two once Recognized Market Operator registration is obtained, but no date is committed and investors should not
subscribe in reliance on it. An investor who may need the money before 5 November 2030 should treat this offering as unsuitable.

Liquidity and working capital risk of the Issuer
The Issuer requires liquidity to fund its operations, service its debt and meet its capital commitments. As at 31 December 2025, the Group
held cash and bank balances of RM10.6 million and recorded a current ratio of 0.64 times. An unexpected lengthening of the collection
cycle, the withdrawal of uncommitted banking lines, or a requirement to post additional performance security could strain the liquidity
position of the Group.

Rating risk

7.2 Risks relating to the industry and operations of the Issuer

Concentration in two assets
The Issuer owns two material shopping centres. Pekan Bandar Baru contributed 61.4 per cent. of FY2025 revenue and Pekan Semenyih
32.2 per cent. There is no diversification of consequence, and no third asset to absorb an adverse event at either.

A fire, flood, prolonged infrastructure failure, loss of an anchor tenant, or the opening of a competing centre in the same catchment would
affect a large proportion of the income of the Issuer. An owner of ten centres would absorb the same event with far less difficulty.

Anchor tenant dependence
Each centre is anchored by a supermarket operator. Anchors occupy substantial space at rentals below the specialty average, and their
value lies in the footfall they generate for the specialty tenants paying higher rents.

The departure of an anchor is more damaging than the rent it represents, because footfall falls across the centre and specialty tenants seek
reductions or exercise break rights. The Bandar Baru anchor lease expires in 2029, within the tenure of these Notes.

Small tenant credit and arrears
Beyond the anchors, the tenant base comprises small businesses: food operators, service providers, clinics and tuition centres. Many are
individual proprietorships with limited capital.

Impairment of trade receivables was RM640,000, RM720,000 and RM810,000 over the review period, rising in each year against revenue
growth. Investors should read this as a normal cost of neighbourhood retail rather than an exceptional item, but should note the direction.

Property valuation and capitalisation rate sensitivity
The centres are carried at fair value, determined principally by capitalising net property income at a rate derived from market evidence, so
value is sensitive to both income and the capitalisation rate.

A rise of fifty basis points in the capitalisation rate applied to the portfolio, with income unchanged, would reduce the carrying value of the
investment properties by approximately RM19.4 million on FY2025 figures, reducing equity correspondingly and taking gross gearing from
1.36 times to approximately 1.72 times, close to the 1.75 times covenant. Capitalisation rates typically rise when interest rates rise and
when retail sentiment weakens, and the same conditions usually depress income.

Refinancing dependence
The Issuer does not repay principal out of earnings. Rental income services interest; principal is refinanced at maturity against property
value. Repayment of these Notes in 2030 depends on a lender or investor being willing to refinance at that time, on the valuation then
prevailing, and on the credit environment.

The Bandar Baru term loan of RM68.0 million matures in 2032 and the Semenyih facility of RM36.0 million in 2031, so the Issuer faces a
sequence of refinancings shortly after the maturity of this issue. A refinancing difficulty on either facility would be visible well before it
affected these Notes, but it would affect them.

Online retail and competing supply
Physical retail competes with online channels, most acutely in fashion, electronics and books. Both centres are weighted toward groceries,
food and beverage and services, which have proved more resilient, but tenant mix must be actively managed and re-let space is not always
taken by a tenant of comparable covenant.

Substantial retail floorspace has been added in the Bangi and Semenyih corridor over the past decade with further centres under
construction. A new centre opening in either catchment would be expected to reduce footfall, occupancy and achievable rents, and the
Issuer has no ability to prevent it.

Asset enhancement execution

What is the coupon rate, and how is it determined?
The coupon is 6.50 per cent. per annum on nominal value, fixed for four years. That is RM3.25 per RM100 held every six months, or
RM6.50 a year. The rate reflects three things: an A3 rating toward the lower end of investment grade, the concentration of the Issuer in
two shopping centres, and interest cover of 2.8 times, which is the thinnest on the Platform.

How often are payments made, and when will I receive them?
Coupon is paid semi-annually in arrears in arrears on 5 May and 5 November of each year, with the first payment falling due on 5 May
2027 and the final payment falling due on the maturity date, 5 November 2030. Payments are made to the persons shown in the records of
the depository at the close of business on the relevant record date, which falls two business days before the payment date. Payments are
credited directly through RENTAS; no physical certificates are issued and no coupons need to be presented.

When will I get my principal back?
The full RM100 per RM100 of nominal value is repaid on 5 November 2030 together with the final coupon. There is no amortisation.
Investors should understand that the Issuer expects to repay by refinancing rather than out of earnings, which is normal for property
investment but means repayment depends on property values and credit availability in 2030.

What happens if the Issuer defaults?
A default arises if the Issuer fails to pay any amount due within the applicable grace period, breaches a financial covenant, breaches
another material obligation and fails to remedy it within the cure period, becomes subject to insolvency proceedings, or triggers one of the
other events of default described in Section 4.9. Upon a continuing event of default, the Trustee may, and must if directed by an
extraordinary resolution of Bondholders, accelerate the Bonds so that the full nominal value and accrued coupon become immediately due
and payable.

The Notes are unsecured, and the difficulty is that RM104.0 million of secured borrowings is already charged over both centres, which
constitute substantially the whole value of the Issuer. Unsecured creditors would look to whatever value exceeds those charges and to the
unencumbered assets maintained under the covenant described in Section 4.6. Shopping centre values are sensitive to occupancy and to
capitalisation rates, both of which typically deteriorate in the circumstances that would produce a default, so a valuation prepared today is
a poor guide to recovery in a distressed sale. The biennial valuation covenant is the mechanism by which investors would learn of
deterioration before it became critical.

Individual investors do not enforce directly. The Trustee acts on behalf of all Bondholders collectively, which prevents a disorderly race to
enforce and ensures that any recoveries are distributed rateably in accordance with the priority of payments set out in the Trust Deed.

Is this issuance Shariah compliant?
No. The Notes are a conventional interest-bearing instrument and are not Shariah compliant. No Shariah adviser has been appointed and
no Shariah pronouncement has been obtained. Investors for whom Shariah compliance is a requirement should choose one of the sukuk
offerings on the Platform instead.

What are the main risks I should focus on?
The Issuer would direct attention to five risks in particular. First, credit risk: repayment depends entirely on the Issuer, and the Bonds
benefit from no third-party guarantee. Second, refinancing risk: the Bonds repay in a single bullet payment at maturity and the Issuer
expects to refinance rather than fully amortise from operating cash flow. Third, coupon rate risk: the rate is fixed, so the market value of
the Bonds will fall if benchmark yields rise. Fourth, liquidity risk: the Bonds are unlisted, secondary market trading may be thin, and
investors should be prepared to hold to maturity. Fifth, the sector-specific risks described in Section 7.2, which for the Issuer principally
concern concentration in two shopping centres, exposure to anchor tenant retention and small tenant credit, sensitivity of property values
to capitalisation rates and occupancy, competition from online retail and newly built centres, dependence on refinancing rather than
amortisation to repay principal, and the illiquidity of investment property in a distressed sale. Section 7 should be read in full.

The Anchor Investor has undertaken to the Trustee to maintain a standing bid for bonds held by retail investors, at a price determined by

Investors should be clear about four limitations:

    The price is not the nominal value. Because the formula responds to prevailing yields, an investor selling into the standing bid when
